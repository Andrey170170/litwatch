litwatch v1: web app design pass

Draft for discussion, 2026-10-11. Nothing here is built. Sketch tabs cycle with ← →.

What the web app is for

One person runs litwatch for one research question. The app is where that person reads what the pipeline found overnight, settles the few cases two models disagree on, corrects the system when it is wrong, and looks things up in a collection of 23,000 papers. Everything else (Notion, Zotero, n8n) is an optional export. The spec (section 4.1, web) lists eight pages; this pass decides how they fit together and what they look like before any is built.

The daily job, with real volumes

The numbers are from the M2 trial (evals/2026-10-09-v1-watch.md) scaled to one night of about 530 to 650 papers. They are what the home screen has to carry.

Each nightAboutWhat the owner does with it
Papers ingested and triaged530–650Nothing. A count is reassurance that the run happened.
Watch candidates graded (two calls each)40–60Nothing, unless graded relevant or above.
Graded relevant or must_read1–5Read the title and both reasons; save or correct; one keystroke.
Split verdicts among those0–2Decide which model was right. This is the one thing only a person can do.
Candidates still ungraded after three failed runsrareLook at it by hand; it must not disappear.
Graded adjacent10–20Skim the titles if there is time. Labelling them is optional evidence.
Graded none25–40Never shown by default. Reachable through search.

So the thing that needs a person is small: five to ten items on a normal morning, a couple of them splits. The screen that carries it should read in two minutes on a phone, and the phone is a real client: the reject sample was labelled on one, and reporting a miss by sharing an arXiv link from a phone is the usual case (spec, decision 4).

The other jobs

  • Weekly reading (M4): one Radar and Pulse review per topic, a Watch recap. Read once, maybe save a pick.
  • Reporting a miss: paste or share a link, then see what each stage said and mark which topic it belonged to.
  • Looking a paper up: its abstract, both opinions, the label, links, BibTeX, a deep card.
  • Searching the collection: 23,000 papers plus the archive, filtered by topic, grade, year, source.
  • Editing a topic: rubric and keywords, with a preview against labelled papers before saving and a scoped re-run after.
  • Checking quality and health: the four recalls per Watch topic over time, open misses, runs, cost, integrations, stuck papers.

Design plan

The three sketches disagree about structure, which is what this pass is for. They share what should not be in question: the semantic colours for grades, the typography principle, and the token system that makes the app re-skinnable for anyone who self-hosts it.

Grades are the one colour code

must read relevant adjacent none split, or ungraded

Must read is ink, not a loud colour: the strongest thing on the page is the paper itself. Relevant is green. Adjacent is a cool grey, visibly not a positive. Amber marks what needs the owner: a split verdict or a candidate grading could not finish. Nothing else on the page is coloured, so a glance down a list answers "is there anything for me". The two Watch topics are told apart by name, not colour; a third topic should not need a new hue.

Typography carries the content

The content is paper titles and two models' reasons about them. Titles are set in a serif in two of the three sketches (Charter, Iowan Old Style, then Georgia; no web font to load), because a serif title reads as a paper, not a notification, and a column of them scans like a reading list. The interface itself (filters, buttons, counts, the reasons) is in the system sans. Sketch B is the deliberate exception: all sans, dense, an instrument.

Tokens, so it can be re-skinned

Each sketch is written against the same CSS variable names, which are shadcn/ui's: --background, --foreground, --muted, --border, --primary, plus four grade colours and one attention colour that litwatch adds. Whoever self-hosts changes one file. The look of a sketch is therefore separable from its structure: pick a layout from one and a palette from another if that is what you want.

Principles the sketches are held to

  • The home screen ends with "nothing else needs you". The recall promise is made visible, not implied.
  • A verdict is never shown without its reasons. Two models graded; the owner sees both sentences, always.
  • One keystroke labels. Keys are the same everywhere: 1 relevant, 2 adjacent, 3 not relevant, 4 unsure, j k move, o open. The phone gets buttons in the same order.
  • Every paper has a URL. A miss trace, a deep card, a split: all of them are one shareable page.
  • Why a paper is here is on the paper: triage's sentence, the keyword that matched, which topic version judged it.
  • No decoration. Rules, colours and marks each encode something listed above or they go.

Material

Every paper in the sketches is real and every grade and reason is what the models actually said in eval run 7 (a dry run over the labelled set on 2026-10-09). They are shown as if they were one night's results. Counts on the pages ("530 papers") are the real 7 October intake.

Decisions to settle before building

Each has the options the sketches embody and a recommendation. The recommendation tab collects them; mark up here.

1. What is the home screen, and how is the app organised?

A and B open on an Inbox: a queue of verdicts that need the owner, with Briefings as a separate page, as the spec lists them. C opens on Today: the night's results written as a dated page, where the verdicts that need attention, the ones that do not, and (from M4) the daily review of interpretability are sections of one document, and the "briefing" is not a separate thing. Yesterday is one page back.

The spec's eight pages were a list of capabilities, not a navigation decision. C spends two pages fewer (Inbox and Briefings become Today and its history) and matches how the thing is actually used: once a day, top to bottom. A and B match how most apps work and keep the Inbox meaningful across several days away. The question is whether litwatch is something you read or something you operate.

2. What does the home screen show by default?

All three agree on the proposal, which differs from "every new verdict": verdicts at relevant or above, every split, every candidate that is ungraded after three failed runs, and nothing at none. Adjacent verdicts are present but folded, as a count that expands to titles. The folded list is where recall insurance gets checked by eye when there is time, and labelling an adjacent paper from there is evidence like any other.

Open point: whether an agreed relevant verdict needs any action to leave the screen. Proposal: no. Items leave when labelled or when the owner marks the day read. Labelling a must-read or relevant paper is one key and is what production precision is measured from, so the screen should make it the easy default without forcing it.

3. How does the owner work through items?

A: a single column, one item expanded at a time, keyboard moves down the list. The current labelling page, grown up. Identical on phone and desktop. B: list on the left, the selected paper on the right, so the abstract and both opinions are always visible while scanning. On a phone it becomes two screens. C: a document; every item that needs a decision is fully expanded already, with its decision row underneath, because there are only a few of them. Nothing to click to see the reasons.

4. How is a split shown and settled?

The reconciliation rule (the more inclusive grade stands, marked split) is fixed by the spec. The design question is presentation. All three sketches put the two opinions side by side, each with its model's name and grade, and make the owner's label the thing that settles it. In A and C the label row sits directly under the two reasons so the choice reads as "who was right"; in B it is in the detail pane's header. A split does not get a separate screen.

5. The phone

What must work well on a phone: the home screen, the paper page, reporting a miss (share target). What must be usable but may be plain: search, labelling from search. Desktop only: the topic editor, Quality, System. Sketch B's three panes are the only structure that needs a second layout for phones; A and C are one layout.

6. The paper page

A full page with its own URL in all three sketches, not a drawer. It is where a miss trace, the deep card, the BibTeX and the history of verdicts across paper revisions and topic versions live, and it has to be linkable from a Notion export or a chat. In B the same component is the right-hand pane; opening it in full is the same URL.

7. Look

Three looks are shown, one per sketch, and are separable from structure. A: cool off-white paper, serif titles, a dark green primary carried over from the plan document. B: cool grey workspace, white panes, all sans, blue primary, thirteen-point type. C: white page, serif prose, the date as the only large thing. Dark mode follows the system in all three through the same tokens.

8. Topic editor and preview

Not contested; one proposal in "Other screens". The one decision inside it: preview cost is shown before the run, not after, and the preview's result is the four recalls over labelled papers next to the current version's numbers.

9. Search

One box, filter chips for topic, grade, year and source, results as a list; a result only the archive holds is marked "outside the collection" with one action. Not contested; B's list and detail is the natural desktop layout for it whichever sketch wins the home screen.

How the rest follows from this structure

  • This week is the same page at week grain: the Watch recap (what was saved, what you overruled), then one review per weekly topic. It is the spec's weekly briefing with the labels on it.
  • Days back are the pager at the top. After time away, Today opens as "Since Thursday" and merges the days, each section still grouped by grade; the per-day pages stay reachable.
  • Paper is a full page in the same type: title, abstract, verdicts per topic with both opinions, history, label, links, BibTeX, publication record, deep card. The entries above are a condensed form of it.
  • Search borrows B's list and detail on the desktop and is a plain list on the phone.
  • Mark the day read is what clears the page; labels are optional on agreed verdicts and required by nothing, but the "Keep as relevant" button is one tap and feeds precision.

Recommendation

Build C's structure with A's item anatomy, and use B's list and detail only where there is volume: Search and the Library on a desktop.

Why C for the home screen

  • The real quantity is five to ten items a morning, two of them decisions. A queue (A) or a desk (B) is built for a backlog that does not exist and makes the small quantity look like work. A page written for the day shows it as what it is: a short read that ends.
  • Litwatch's promise is recall. C's last line, "that is everything from the night of 7 October", is that promise stated every day. Neither Inbox sketch has a natural place to say it.
  • The split is the one decision only a person can make, and C puts the two sentences and the "who is right?" row in the reading flow, not behind a selection or an expansion.
