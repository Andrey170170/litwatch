litwatch v1: web app design

Draft for discussion, 2026-10-11, third pass. Nothing here is built.

What the web app is

It is the user side of litwatch: the place where the value the system produces is handed over. Litwatch reads five to six hundred papers a night so that one person does not have to, and the web app is how that person gets the result: what is new in their field, which papers matter for their question, and enough about each to decide whether to read it. The pipeline that produces this (triage, keyword audit, two graders, reconciliation, runs, integrations) is the kitchen. It is reachable, through Settings, but it is not on the table.

The first draft of this document framed the app as a decision queue: an Inbox, splits that "need you", labels with one keystroke. That was the spec's wording taken literally, and it is wrong. On a normal morning nothing needs the owner. They read.

How it is used

  • Every morning, usually on a phone: open the Digest and read the latest issue like a newspaper. First the research questions (the Watch topics): the new papers that matter, each with a short written take. Then the field: the daily review of interpretability. On Mondays a weekly issue carries the weekly reviews and a recap. It ends, and says so.
  • Through the week: come back to this week's reviews, pinned above the feed, and scroll back through earlier issues when something rings a bell.
  • When a paper looks good: open it. Abstract, litwatch's take, the deep card, links to arXiv and the PDF, save to the library, copy BibTeX. One tap each, with the pen already there.
  • When working on a topic: open its page and go through every paper the topic has found, as a table, sorted and filtered as needed. That is the reading list for current research.
  • When looking for something: Papers. One table over the whole collection with a search box; the arxiv-archive joins the search when it is connected. One of the possible outcomes of a search is "the automation missed this".
  • Occasionally: glance at the status line to see that the night went fine; thumb a paper up or down to tell the chef; open Settings to edit a topic, check quality, or look at an integration.

Principles the screens follow

PrincipleWhat it rules out
One voice. Litwatch speaks as itself: "this paper does X; it matters for your question because Y." Grade names, judge and check, split, model names, reason strings. Those exist, under Settings. A disagreement is shown at the more inclusive grade with no mark.
Written where it is read, tabular where it is browsed. The Digest is prose with headlines; a topic is a table. Verdict rows on the Digest; prose where a sortable list is wanted.
Offered, never asked. Feedback is always available and never requested. Label queues, "mark as read" to clear, anything that accumulates as a backlog.
All quiet, at a glance. One line says whether the run finished and whether the graders seriously disagreed. Run logs, costs and recall on the front. The line opens the quality page in Settings.
Kitchen behind a door. Topic configuration, quality, runs, integrations, misses are Settings pages. Quality and System as top-level navigation.
Self-contained. With no integration connected, every page works: search is litwatch's own, PDFs link to arXiv, published versions come from DBLP in the core, the deep card section is absent rather than a dead button. Any page that assumes Nextcloud, the archive, n8n or a deep-card provider. Lab mates will run it bare.
Phone first, desktop whole. One column on the phone; on a desktop the width is used for a reading pane and real tables. A desktop that is a stretched phone, or a phone that is a cut-down desktop.

Navigation

Three places and a gear. Digest, Topics, Papers: a footer with three buttons on the phone, three words in the header on a desktop. The gear holds Topics (edit, preview, re-run), Quality, Misses, Runs, Integrations, Configuration. Papers absorbs Search and the Library: it is the whole collection as a table with a search box, and Saved is a filter on it. The levels are Digest → issue → paper and Topics → topic → paper, each one tap apart.

Look

Three candidates, same structure. Quiet is pure white with system fonts and nothing but type. Masthead is the proposal: warm paper, a newspaper masthead with a double rule, the status line set as the printer's line under it, section headings with a short rule in a colour per topic (green for CoT monitoring, oxblood for CoT faithfulness, indigo for interpretability, ochre for agents), serif for the takes as well as the titles. The topic colours carry over to the topic chips and the topic pages, so a colour means a topic everywhere. Dark is Masthead on a dark ground, for the phone at night and for anyone whose Notion looks like Andrei's. All three are one token file apart; the structure does not move. The rest of this document is drawn in Masthead.

What is on the page, and what is not

  • The masthead carries the date of the open issue and the printer's line: how many papers were read and whether all is quiet. Amber with a count when the graders seriously disagreed; red "last night's run failed" when there is no issue. Tapping the line opens Quality.
  • This week is the pinned row: the weekly issue's sections, one tap each, there from Monday to Sunday. The next Monday replaces it.
  • The lede is written by the writer from the night's verdicts, in one breath. It is the only place counts appear outside the printer's line.
  • Sections are the Watch topics in the owner's order, then the daily Radar topics. Each has its colour on the rule. A topic with nothing new is one line, never an empty section.
  • Lead entries are the papers graded must read: a two or three sentence take. Brief entries are the relevant ones: one sentence. A paper the graders disagreed on is shown at the more inclusive grade and looks like any other. Adjacent papers are not here; the fold line counts them and sends you to the topic pages.
  • The action row is the pen: Read, arXiv, PDF, Save, BibTeX; Saved is a state. The thumbs sit at the end, quiet until used.
  • Earlier is the feed: every issue back in time, folded to date and lede. A weekly issue is marked. Month links and a date picker are for going far back.
  • Weekend. No run with content, no issue. The masthead reads "Saturday 11 October · nothing new since Friday" over Friday's issue.
  • No "mark read". The issue ends when it ends.

How this was chosen

▲▼Tell the chef

Decisions on this page

  • Take above abstract. The take is written for the owner and is why the paper is here; the abstract is the authors' and comes second. Both are short; the deep card is the long read.
  • Deep card is present for must-read papers (the spec's deep integration requests one on that grade) and otherwise a single button, "Request a deep card", in its place. The card's sections come from the integration; the page renders what it is given.
  • BibTeX copies to the clipboard with the published version's venue when DBLP has one, otherwise the arXiv entry. Save adds to the library and is what Zotero and the other integrations react to.
  • Topic chips say which topics this paper belongs to. A paper can be in several; it gets one take per topic, each under its chip, and the card names each topic it speaks to.
  • How this was chosen unfolds to: the triage decision, the two graders' grades and reasons by model name, the reconciled grade, and the owner's label if any. That is the content the old Inbox put first. Here it is last and closed.

How a topic is picked

  • One screen. Five to ten topics is the realistic range for anyone, so the tab is the full list, grouped Watch, Radar, Pulse in the same order as the Digest's sections. No search box, no folders.
  • Each card: the topic's colour as a bar on the left, its name, its kind and cadence in small type, one line of what is new since you last opened it, and counts on the right. "Nothing new" is grey; a paused topic sinks to the bottom and fades, still openable.
  • One tap opens the topic page: the table for a Watch topic, the reviews with the table under them for Radar and Pulse.
  • No edit controls on the cards. Creating, editing, pausing and reordering are Settings → Topics, reached by the gear.
  • Desktop replaces the cards with a rail: the same list down the left, with the chosen topic open beside it, so switching topics is one click and never leaves the table. Opening the Topics tab lands on the first topic, or the one you had open last.

Search, and the archive

  • Litwatch's own search is titles and abstracts over its database, and it is what a lab mate with nothing connected gets. It is the baseline the page must work on.
  • The archive joins automatically when its integration is connected (spec, decision 6): every query goes to it too, through the search provider protocol, and its hits merge by arXiv id. Where a result comes from is a filter beside topic and year, with no default restriction. An archive-only paper shows as outside the collection with Fetch as its action.
  • Ranking. Both sides are lexical today, so the merged list is newest first, which is honest about what the search is. When the archive gains embeddings, the protocol grows an optional score and the page gains a "best match" sort that orders by the provider's score where there is one. Semantic search is not built into litwatch; it is where the papers are, and the archive has all of them.
  • Filters only litwatch knows (topic, saved) apply to collection papers; archive-only hits stay in their own band under the filtered rows.
  • Reporting a miss is the amber link on a row. It asks which topic, takes an optional note, and the trace of what each stage said goes to Settings → Misses. On the phone the Android share target lands on this same flow with the link already looked up.
  • Without the search box, Papers is simply the whole collection, newest first, with Saved as the filter that makes it the library. Export BibTeX is on the filter row.

The other Settings pages, in words

  • Topics: the list of topics with kind, cadence, revision and status, and the editor from the first draft (subject, rubric, keywords with match counts; preview a draft against labelled papers and misses; scoped re-runs after saving; pause).
  • Quality is what the chip opens. At the top, last night: the disagreements with both opinions and the label row, so a person who wants to settle them can. Below, per Watch topic: the four recalls and precision over evaluation runs, each number a link to its papers; the correction rate from thumbs; and a "draw a calibration sample" button that puts ten random candidates on the existing labelling page for a precision figure that is not biased by what the owner chose to react to.
  • Misses: reported misses with their stage traces (the first draft's trace page), open and resolved.
  • Runs and cost: a row per night with counts, duration, cost by role and provider, errors; the month's total; papers triage is stuck on, with a dismiss.
  • Configuration: the loaded litwatch.toml as read, secrets masked, with the models in use per role and the lab rule's verdict. Read-only in v1; the file stays the source of truth, which keeps the API free of a config-write path and the token's power bounded.
  • Integration cards are the generic rendering of the Integration protocol's status: enabled, a one-line description the module supplies, cursor lag, failures with the last error, retry and skip. A module that also implements SearchProvider reports its answer rate and latency. A new integration gets a card without any frontend work.

Pipeline: the writer

The Digest needs written takes, so the pipeline gains one step after reconciliation: for each paper graded relevant or above, a writer produces the take (two or three sentences for must read, one for relevant) from the abstract, the two graders' reasons and the topic's subject, in litwatch's voice and addressed to the owner. Once a night it also writes the lede from the day's verdicts. A mid-sized model (Sonnet 5.5 as the proposal, settled by evaluation like the other roles); at one to five papers a night the cost is cents. The take is stored on the verdict and regenerated only when the verdict changes. This belongs in M3, since the page has nothing to say without it, and it is the same shape as the M4 review writer, which can share its prompt scaffolding. The weekly issue's Watch recap is also the writer's.

Feedback model

Thumbs are stored as reactions (paper, topic, value up or down, reason, time). The reason "unrelated to the topic" also writes a label (not relevant, origin reaction) and "great find" writes relevant, origin reaction; a label set on the paper page has origin reader and outranks them; the other reasons do not touch labels. Opening a paper page and saving are recorded as events already (paper.saved exists; paper.opened is new and local). Quality reports the correction rate from reactions and keeps precision honest with the calibration sample rather than from reactions, which are biased by construction. The existing labelling page stays as the evaluation tool.

Search

Decision 6 stands. Two additions: the SearchProvider hit gains an optional score, and the merged list is sorted newest first until a provider supplies scores, when a "best match" sort appears. Semantic search is explicitly the archive's job, not litwatch's.

API

/inbox becomes /digest (issues, daily and weekly, with sections and takes). /feedback gains reactions. /papers search gains the source filter and the provider score. /integrations grows the status shape the cards render (description, lag, failures, retry, skip, search provider stats). /library stays as the saved set behind the Saved filter. Everything else stands.
